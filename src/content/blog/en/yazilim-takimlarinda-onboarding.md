I've changed jobs quite a few times throughout my career. As a result, I've repeatedly found myself being "the new person" in different companies, different cultures, and completely different teams.

Sometimes, I joined a project that was just getting started and had to begin by understanding the domain. Other times, I joined an established codebase that had been growing for years and had to figure out both the code and the business behind it.

In this post, I want to share some of what I've learned from those experiences. Of course, onboarding can look very different depending on the size and structure of a company. I'll look at the process from both sides: **what you can do to ramp up faster when you're joining a new team**, and **what you can do to make life easier for someone who is about to join your team**.

## If You're Joining a New Team

If you're joining an existing codebase, things can be a little more challenging. You have to understand both the code and the business at the same time.

If you're lucky, you'll have a **buddy**.

If you do, one of the first things you should do is stop worrying about "bothering" them.

I'm not saying you should ask your buddy about every little problem. But if there's something you can't find in the documentation or some logic you simply can't figure out, don't hesitate to ask.

Sometimes, something you could learn by asking a one-minute question can easily consume an entire day if you try to figure it out by yourself.

Of course, there's a trade-off here. Spending time investigating things on your own can teach you a lot as well. The important thing is finding the right balance.

One of the most effective ways to understand a new codebase is to **follow a feature from beginning to end**.

Look at where a request enters the API, which layers it passes through, which services become involved, and where and how the data is processed.

Following a complete flow like this can help you build a mental model of the architecture.

At first, the filenames, folder structures, and modules may all look confusing. But once you follow a flow end to end, you gradually start seeing the structure inside what initially looked like chaos.

(And if there really is no structure, well... things might be a little harder.)

If you've joined an established team, you'll probably receive plenty of comments on your first few Pull Requests.

Those comments aren't necessarily there because you're a bad developer. They're often there because the team has its own standards and conventions.

As you gradually internalize those standards, your work naturally starts fitting into the team's way of doing things, and the number of review comments usually decreases.

So **don't take comments on your first PRs personally**.

Instead, think of them as part of learning the team's language.

I also don't think the approach of saying, "Just spend your first few weeks getting familiar with everything; we won't give you any work yet," is particularly effective.

At some point, real learning starts by doing real work.

That's why I think it's important to receive a **real task**, even if it's a small one, starting from your first sprint.

Try to solve it yourself first. When you get stuck, ask the right questions and get support.

This helps you understand both the codebase and the domain much faster.

When someone explains a system or concept to you for the first time, there's a good chance that very little of it will actually stick.

But once you work on something related to it, open a PR, and receive feedback, you suddenly reach that moment of:

"Oh, now I get it."

While reading the code, you'll inevitably come across places where you think:

"Why was this written like this?"

or even:

**"This is not how this should be done."**

As someone looking at the system with fresh eyes, it's natural to notice these things.

But it's important to remember that the circumstances were probably very different when that code was written.

Maybe there was a tight deadline.

Maybe the team structure was different.

Maybe that technology choice made perfect sense at the time.

In many cases, what you're seeing isn't necessarily a "bad decision." It's a decision that was made under a different set of circumstances.

So rather than criticizing things too quickly, it's usually better to understand why they exist first and then suggest improvements at the appropriate time.

Another way to accelerate your learning is to **work on different types of tasks**.

The more areas of the project you touch, the fewer unknowns remain.

There will also be people on the team who are clearly the experts in certain areas.

Pair programming with these people on some of their tasks can be incredibly valuable.

Watching how they approach a problem, where they add logs, which tools they use for debugging, and how they identify risky areas can help you develop instincts that would otherwise take much longer to build.

Onboarding isn't only technical.

**Building social connections with the team** is just as important as understanding the code.

If you work remotely, join virtual coffee breaks. Ask someone if they want to spend 15 minutes having a coffee and chatting.

If you're in an office, join casual conversations in the kitchen or tag along during breaks, even if you're not going out for the same reason as everyone else.

These small interactions help you get to know your teammates.

Once you start feeling like you're actually part of the team, asking questions, sharing opinions, and staying calm when you make a mistake all become much easier.

When you join your first few planning meetings, you'll probably understand very little of what's going on.

You'll hear unfamiliar abbreviations, references to things that happened months ago, customer names, and domain-specific terminology flying around the room.

There's no need to panic and think, "I don't understand anything."

That's a completely normal part of the process.

Eventually, without even realizing it, sentences that once sounded completely foreign will start making perfect sense.

**If you're joining a brand-new project**, you won't have a frightening legacy codebase waiting for you, but that doesn't necessarily make things easier.

In fact, it comes with a different kind of responsibility.

You need to understand the domain, design the architecture together with your teammates, and decide which technologies and patterns you're going to use and how you're going to use them.

The decisions made at the beginning of a project can either become technical baggage that you carry for years or a solid foundation that makes everyone's life easier.

That's why it's important not to avoid technical discussions.

Come to meetings prepared.

Instead of simply saying:

"I think we should do it this way,"

try to say:

"I think this approach makes more sense because..."

## If Someone Is Joining Your Team

Now let's look at the other side.

You might be expecting someone new to join your team, and naturally, you want them to adapt quickly and become productive as soon as possible.

One of the most important things here is **standards**.

Having consistent standards across the codebase makes life significantly easier for newcomers.

Folder structures, naming conventions, patterns, service structures, and model configurations shouldn't change depending on who happened to build a particular part of the system.

When these things follow a consistent structure, newcomers gradually learn where certain types of logic are likely to live.

That predictability can dramatically speed up the learning process.

Standards aren't limited to architecture or folder structures either.

Having proper linting and formatting rules also makes a big difference during code reviews.

Automated formatters, linting rules, and pre-commit hooks reduce comments such as:

"Format this."

"There's a spacing issue here."

This allows code reviews to focus on things that actually matter: architecture, readability, testing, and business rules.

**PLEASE DON'T DO THIS: DON'T BUILD YOUR OWN INTERNAL FRAMEWORK FOR THE COMPANY. IT PROBABLY WON'T HAVE GOOD DOCUMENTATION. NEWCOMERS WON'T UNDERSTAND ANYTHING. AND WHEN THE PERSON WHO BUILT THE FRAMEWORK LEAVES, THE PROJECT WILL FALL APART.**

I also think ignoring AI tools today is a missed opportunity.

If your team uses a **coding agent such as Cursor**, or another AI-assisted development tool, it can be particularly useful for someone who's just joined.

A newcomer can start by asking the tool about a function they don't understand, the responsibility of a particular service, or a specific domain rule.

They can ask it to summarize what a section of code does or explain parts of the architecture.

If you're using Cursor, shared team-level **Rules** and **Commands** can also be very useful.

Assigning a **buddy** to a newcomer can significantly accelerate the onboarding process.

The buddy becomes the natural first person they can go to with questions during the day.

They can help with everything from setting up the development environment and getting repository access to choosing the first task and understanding the code review process.

When choosing a buddy, it's important to find someone who is technically strong but also patient and comfortable communicating with others.

It's equally important to make the buddy role visible.

Instead of treating it as invisible extra work, acknowledge it explicitly:

"This is also part of your responsibilities right now, so let's make sure you have time for it."

That makes expectations clearer and shows that the onboarding process is being taken seriously.

**Good onboarding documentation is essential.**

Saying, "There was a Confluence page somewhere; you'll find it," isn't really onboarding.

A good onboarding document should answer the question a newcomer has when they open their laptop on day one:

**"What am I supposed to do now?"**

Which tools need to be installed?

Which repositories do they need access to?

What steps are required to run the project locally?

Which environment variables are needed?

How does the team's branching strategy work?

What is expected during the PR process?

What are the basic concepts they need to understand about the domain?

Having all of this information organized in one place is incredibly valuable.

Keeping that documentation alive is just as important as creating it in the first place.

Actively ask newcomers for feedback and even **encourage them to improve the onboarding documentation themselves**.

This both gets them involved in the process and helps keep the documentation up to date.

Uncertainty is one of the most exhausting things for someone who's just started a new job.

"What should I be doing today?"

"Am I being productive enough?"

"What should I be looking into next?"

Questions like these can make an already confusing process even more stressful.

A clear plan for the first few days and the first week can help a lot.

For example:

The first day can focus mostly on introductions, environment setup, and a general overview.

The second day might include a tour of the codebase, reading some important documentation, and examining a simple endpoint.

After that, the newcomer can start working on a small, low-risk task that still provides real value.

This is useful both technically and psychologically.

Even a short five- or ten-minute conversation at the end of the day asking, "How's everything going?" can make someone feel much less alone during their first few days.

The first task you give a newcomer also deserves some thought.

Giving them only superficial work, such as fixing documentation, can sometimes create the impression that:

"They don't trust me with real work yet."

On the other hand, assigning something extremely critical that could potentially break production isn't a great introduction either.

Ideally, you want a well-designed **good first task**.

It should allow the newcomer to touch the real codebase and, if possible, work with part of an end-to-end flow while keeping the risk manageable.

Successfully completing a task like this builds confidence and reinforces the feeling:

"I'm actually part of this team now."

Onboarding also shouldn't be limited to documentation and tasks.

It's important to provide architecture and domain knowledge in a more structured way.

A short **Architecture Overview** session can help newcomers understand what the different parts of the system are responsible for.

Similarly, a **Domain 101** session explaining the business side, key concepts, and important rules gives them a strong foundation for understanding future conversations.

Recording these sessions and making them reusable for future newcomers can save the team a significant amount of time in the long run.

Especially during the first few weeks, it's important not only to say:

**"You can ask anything that comes to mind. If we don't know the answer, we'll figure it out together."**

You also need to demonstrate that attitude through your actions.

A culture that encourages questions doesn't just accelerate the newcomer's learning. It improves the learning speed of the entire team.

Finally, don't forget about **social onboarding**.

A first day spent entirely talking about technical topics can be surprisingly forgettable.

Have a coffee together.

If you're working from an office, organize a casual lunch.

If the team is remote, arrange a short online introduction session.

Even a simple introduction message in Slack or Teams can help people discover shared interests.

Onboarding that doesn't forget the human side tends to make the technical side much smoother as well.

## Final Thoughts

Changing jobs frequently has given me the opportunity to experience **onboarding** many times from both perspectives: as the person joining a new team and as someone welcoming a newcomer.

Each time, one thing has become clearer to me:

Good onboarding isn't just:

"Set everything up, read the documentation, and let's see how it goes."

It's a deliberately designed process with both technical and human aspects.

As a newcomer, being patient, asking questions without hesitation, getting involved in real work as early as possible, working on different types of tasks, and building social connections with the team can dramatically accelerate the learning process.

From the team's perspective, clear standards, solid onboarding documentation, a buddy system, effective use of AI tools, a well-planned first week, and a culture where **asking questions is always welcome** can help a newcomer become comfortable and productive weeks earlier.
